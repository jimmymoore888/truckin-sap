## ADA / EEO ISSUE FOR REVIEW

The ADA prohibits covered employers from discriminating against a qualified applicant on the basis of disability in hiring and other employment decisions. The ADA also addresses discrimination involving an actual disability, a record of impairment, or circumstances in which an individual is regarded as having an impairment. (EEOC)

Of particular significance, the ADA prohibits the use of qualification standards, employment tests, or other selection criteria that screen out or tend to screen out an individual or class of individuals with disabilities unless the employer can establish that the challenged standard is job-related for the position and consistent with business necessity. (EEOC)

My concern is therefore whether a categorical rule such as:

“Anyone who has ever been through SAP is permanently ineligible for employment”

is being used as a legitimate present-day qualification requirement, or whether historical SAP participation is being used as a proxy for a past, recorded, or perceived substance-use impairment.

That distinction appears legally significant.

The ADA does not protect current illegal drug use when an employer acts on the basis of that current use. However, federal ADA provisions expressly distinguish individuals who have successfully completed rehabilitation and are no longer engaging in illegal drug use, individuals participating in supervised rehabilitation while no longer engaging in such use, and certain individuals erroneously regarded as currently engaging in such use. (EEOC)

SAFETY AND “DIRECT THREAT” CONSIDERATIONS

I recognize that commercial motor vehicle operation is safety-sensitive employment and that trucking companies have legitimate responsibilities concerning public safety.

However, if disability is implicated and a carrier contends that my historical SAP participation establishes a present safety threat, I request examination of whether the employer conducted the individualized assessment contemplated by ADA standards.

EEOC guidance states that a direct-threat determination must address the individual’s present ability to safely perform the essential functions of the job and must rely on current medical knowledge and/or the best available objective evidence rather than speculative or generalized assumptions. (EEOC)

There is historical EEOC enforcement directly relevant to this question. In litigation involving Exxon’s policy concerning workers with records of past substance abuse in safety-sensitive positions, the EEOC stated that consideration of past substance-abuse history in a direct-threat analysis requires an individualized assessment based upon medical analysis or other objective factual evidence concerning the person’s current ability to perform safely. (EEOC)

My subsequent commercial-driving history is therefore particularly relevant.

I am not merely claiming that I am theoretically capable of driving.

I am actively performing the same safety-sensitive commercial driving occupation from which these prospective employers have allegedly excluded me because of historical SAP participation.

DOT COMPLIANCE DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY REQUIRE HIRING — BUT ADA REQUIREMENTS REMAIN RELEVANT

I understand that successful completion of the federal Return-to-Duty process does not independently compel a particular trucking company to hire or reinstate a driver.

Under 49 C.F.R. § 40.305, satisfying the RTD conditions does not itself require an employer to return an individual to safety-sensitive duties. (eCFR)

Accordingly, I am not asserting that FMCSA regulations create an unconditional right to employment.

Rather, my question is whether an employer remains subject to independent federal employment-discrimination requirements when making that personnel decision and, specifically, whether a permanent categorical exclusion based upon prior SAP history unlawfully discriminates where that history is being used as a proxy for an actual, recorded, or perceived disability.

## POTENTIAL SYSTEMIC ISSUE

The fact that substantially similar policies appear across multiple major trucking companies raises an additional concern beyond an isolated hiring decision.

The EEOC defines systemic discrimination as involving a pattern, practice, policy, or class issue having a broad impact on an industry, profession, company, or geographic area. (EEOC)

I therefore request evaluation of whether the evidence presents only individual refusal-to-hire claims or whether the repeated use of categorical former-SAP exclusions could warrant referral to or investigation by the EEOC as a broader systemic employment practice.
